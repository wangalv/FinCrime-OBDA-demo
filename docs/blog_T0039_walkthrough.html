Beyond Text-to-SQL: Building an AML Semantic Layer for Trustworthy AI-Assisted Investigations

Combining LLM reasoning, Ontology and Ontology-Based Data Access (OBDA) to make financial crime investigations more explainable, traceable and evidence-driven.
Working draft · Architecture / Engineering Blog · with the T0039 example at code level
The central proposition: use LLMs to interpret human intent and handle ambiguity, while using an explicit semantic model, governed data access and deterministic rules to retrieve and evaluate evidence.

1. The problem: what does an AML investigation actually look like?

Imagine receiving an alert involving a handful of suspicious transactions. An investigator examines the transactions, identifies connected accounts and parties, reviews historical activity, looks for relationships, and tests whether the observed behaviour aligns with known financial-crime typologies or risk indicators.

This is an exercise in gathering evidence, connecting relationships and testing hypotheses. It also involves human judgement: investigators work with incomplete information, navigate different data sources and decide what to investigate next. The process can be time-consuming, and outcomes may vary with experience and interpretation.

An LLM could help interpret observations, identify relevant typologies, retrieve supporting evidence and explain findings. But a plausible explanation is not the same as an established fact. In an AML context, the system must make clear what is supported by evidence, what is inferred, and what cannot be established with the available data.

2. The role of LLMs: three modes of reasoning

ModeWhat it doesPotential AML role
DeductiveDerives conclusions from stated premises and rules.Evaluate whether observed transactions satisfy explicit indicator criteria.
InductiveGeneralises patterns from examples; conclusions are probabilistic.Learn suspicious patterns from historical cases or labelled activity.
AbductiveSuggests plausible explanations for observations.Interpret an investigator's narrative and suggest typologies worth exploring.
My proposition is to use LLMs for interpretation and ambiguity handling, while using semantic models, governed data access and deterministic rules for evidence retrieval and evaluation. The objective is not to remove the LLM, but to constrain the decisions that require consistency and traceability.

3. The solution: introducing a semantic layer

Organisations often have business glossaries, data dictionaries, regulatory definitions, rules and domain knowledge, but these resources are disconnected. A transaction database may expose fields such as from_account, to_account, ts and amount_log. An investigator thinks in terms of Originator, Beneficiary, Funds Transfer and Financial Crime Indicators. These vocabularies are related, but they are not interchangeable.

A semantic layer introduces an abstraction between business meaning and physical data. Instead of asking an LLM to discover database structures and generate arbitrary SQL, the application expresses requirements using governed business concepts. The semantic and mapping layers resolve those concepts against the available data.

For this experiment, the implementation combines RDF/OWL and Turtle for the semantic vocabulary, R2RML for relational-to-RDF mappings, Ontop for OBDA and virtual knowledge graph access, and SHACL plus deterministic application rules for validation and evaluation.

4. Solution architecture: six semantic and execution layers

The design separates external knowledge, canonical AML concepts, typology-specific requirements, data semantics, physical mappings and execution. This is a project-specific logical architecture built around OBDA; it is not a claim that OBDA standards prescribe six layers.

Figure 1. The six layers (left) and the artefact each one contributes to the T0039 example (right, dashed). Layers 2–5 form the semantic and data-access foundation; layer 6 consumes and validates the results.

The rest of this post follows one question through these layers. Each layer below shows the file it lives in and the lines that the T0039 example actually uses. All code is copied from the working demo, not rewritten for the post.

Layer 1 — External Knowledge

AMLTRIX provides published typologies, techniques, indicators, data sources and their relationships. The external source remains authoritative; an adapter supports integration without treating the external ontology as the local domain model.

For T0039, the typology gives nine indicators. The first reads: “Cyclical patterns in funds or asset transfers that ultimately revert to the same origin across multiple accounts or entities, lacking a legitimate business rationale.” The demo reads these facts through Ontop, from the typology's own CSV files, with a generated R2RML mapping. The typology is never copied into our files.

Layer 2 — AML Core Domain Ontology

Defines canonical AML concepts and relationships independently of a particular database or typology. The model should distinguish parties from accounts: an originator is not necessarily the same thing as the account from which funds are sent.

ontology/core/aml_core.ttl · 18 AML terms, 7 the data can answer
amd:FundsTransfer a owl:Class ; rdfs:comment "A transfer of funds from one account to another." .
amd:originator a owl:ObjectProperty ; rdfs:domain amd:FundsTransfer ; rdfs:range amd:Account ;
    rdfs:comment "The account the funds leave (the paying account)." .
amd:Jurisdiction a owl:Class ; rdfs:comment "The country or legal territory a transfer or an entity belongs to. The data has no country." .
The vocabulary the indicators talk about. It names no database and no typology. amd:Jurisdiction is declared although the data has no country: that is deliberate (see layer 4).

Honest note: in this draft model, amd:originator points at an account. The party-versus-account distinction above is a design goal, not yet in the demo.

Layer 3 — Typology Requirements

Connects indicators to the AML concepts and contextual information needed for investigation. Requirements should reference the AML core vocabulary, not source-specific terms, so typology knowledge remains independent of physical data models.

ontology/application/typology_requirements.ttl · the Alignment part
amx:IND02191 align:needsTerm amd:FundsTransfer , amd:originator , amd:beneficiary , amd:transferTime .
amx:IND02195 align:needsTerm amd:FundsTransfer , amd:sameInstitution , amd:Jurisdiction .

amx:T0039 align:backgroundTerm fcd:customerType , fcd:industry , fcd:onboardingTenureDays , fcd:pepFlag , fcd:riskRating .
IND02191 needs a funds transfer, who paid, who received, and when. IND02195 needs the same-bank flag and a jurisdiction. Account background is kept apart: no rule reads it.

Layer 4 — Data Semantic and Alignment

Describes source-side concepts and how they correspond to AML concepts. Source vocabulary and semantic alignment are separate responsibilities, even if initially maintained within one logical layer.

ontology/alignment/aml_data_alignment.ttl · the whole bridge, 7 axioms
fcd:Transaction rdfs:subClassOf amd:FundsTransfer .
fcd:Account rdfs:subClassOf amd:Account .
fcd:txId rdfs:subPropertyOf amd:transferId .
fcd:timestamp rdfs:subPropertyOf amd:transferTime .
fcd:fromAccount rdfs:subPropertyOf amd:originator .
fcd:toAccount rdfs:subPropertyOf amd:beneficiary .
fcd:sameBank rdfs:subPropertyOf amd:sameInstitution .
Only sub-class and sub-property, so Ontop can use them for rewriting (OWL 2 QL). An AML term with no line here is one the data cannot answer.

The database terms themselves (fcd:fromAccount and its siblings) are declared next to it in source_vocabulary.ttl. They change when the database changes; the AML terms above them do not.

Layer 5 — OBDA Mapping

R2RML and Ontop connect the semantic vocabulary to relational tables, columns and SQL. The demo uses virtual access: the source data remains in DuckDB rather than being copied into a separate RDF store.

mappings/transaction/tx_features.r2rml.ttl · the only file that names tables and columns
map:TransactionMap a rr:TriplesMap ;
    align:realisesSource amx:DS0019 , amx:DS0047 ;
    rr:logicalTable [ rr:tableName "tx" ] ;
    rr:subjectMap [ rr:template "https://example.org/transaction/{tx_id}" ; rr:class fcd:Transaction ] ;
    rr:predicateObjectMap
        [ rr:predicate fcd:txId ;        rr:objectMap [ rr:column "tx_id" ; rr:datatype xsd:integer ] ] ,
        [ rr:predicate fcd:timestamp ;   rr:objectMap [ rr:column "ts" ; rr:datatype xsd:dateTime ] ] ,
        [ rr:predicate fcd:fromAccount ; rr:objectMap [ rr:template "https://example.org/account/{from_account}" ; rr:termType rr:IRI ] ] ,
        [ rr:predicate fcd:toAccount ;   rr:objectMap [ rr:template "https://example.org/account/{to_account}" ; rr:termType rr:IRI ] ] ,
        [ rr:predicate fcd:amountLog ;   rr:objectMap [ rr:column "amount_log" ; rr:datatype xsd:double ] ] ,
        [ rr:predicate fcd:sameBank ;    rr:objectMap [ rr:column "is_same_bank" ; rr:datatype xsd:boolean ] ] .
A transfer becomes an IRI built from tx_id; fcd:fromAccount reads the column from_account.
mappings/lenses/tx_features.lens.json · a view that declares tx_id unique
{"name": ["tx"], "baseRelation": ["tx_features"], "type": "BasicLens",
 "uniqueConstraints": {"added": [{"name": "tx_pk", "determinants": ["tx_id"]}]}}
Without this, Ontop joins the table to itself once for each property.

Layer 6 — Validation and Execution

SHACL checks graph structure and constraints; indicator-specific code evaluates business logic; the application records outcomes and supporting evidence. Deterministic execution improves repeatability, but does not by itself prove that a rule is a correct AML interpretation.

validation/shapes/t0039.shapes.ttl · two of the seven rules
    # [read] "cyclical pattern": each leg pays from the account the previous leg paid to
    sh:sparql [
        a sh:SPARQLConstraint ; sh:prefixes ex: ;
        sh:message "[consecutive_legs_connect] Consecutive legs must connect: a leg starts at the account the previous leg paid to." ;
        sh:select """
            SELECT $this ?value WHERE {
                $this ex:leg ?a , ?b .
                ?a ex:seq ?s1 ; ex:to ?value .
                ?b ex:seq ?s2 ; ex:from ?f .
                FILTER (?s2 = ?s1 + 1 && ?value != ?f)

    # [added] the funds move forward in time (equal timestamps pass: the data has minute resolution; ties are ordered by tx_id before this check)
    sh:sparql [
        a sh:SPARQLConstraint ; sh:prefixes ex: ;
        sh:message "[legs_in_time_order] Legs must be in time order: a leg cannot happen before the one it follows." ;
        sh:select """
            SELECT $this ?value WHERE {
                $this ex:leg ?a , ?b .
                ?a ex:seq ?s1 ; ex:at ?t1 .
                ?b ex:seq ?s2 ; ex:at ?value .
                FILTER (?s2 = ?s1 + 1 && ?value < ?t1)
Each rule's message starts with [rule_id]; tags [text], [read], [added] say whether the typology says it, we read it in, or we added it.

5. Walking through the demo: T0039 Circular Transactions

The worked example uses AMLTRIX technique T0039 — Circular Transactions. The investigator describes the suspected behaviour in natural language: “The alert flagged these transfers. The money seems to go round in a circle and come back to where it started.” Three transaction IDs are supplied: 2131053, 2204314 and 2282865.

Figure 2. The path of one question. The coloured tags show which layer Ontop reads at each point.

Step 1 — Intent identification

The LLM interprets the narrative and proposes T0039 as the relevant technique. The proposed selection is checked against the published typology catalogue by code: the id must exist and sit under the tactic the model named. Where intent is ambiguous, the application asks for clarification or presents candidate techniques.

Step 2 — Requirement resolution

The application retrieves the indicators associated with T0039 and resolves their declared requirements. For IND02191, the worked model uses FundsTransfer, Originator, Beneficiary and TransferTime concepts. The LLM's only other job is to write the query below, which names the technique and the ids and nothing else. Code rejects a query that names a data term itself (amd:originator, fcd:fromAccount).

PREFIX amlo: <https://example.org/amlo#>
PREFIX skos: <http://www.w3.org/2004/02/skos/core#>
PREFIX align: <https://example.org/align#>
PREFIX amx: <https://example.org/amltrix/>
PREFIX amd: <https://example.org/aml-data#>
SELECT DISTINCT ?ind ?txid ?term ?v WHERE {
  amx:T0039 amlo:isIndicatedBy ?i .
  ?i skos:prefLabel ?ind ; align:needsTerm ?term .
  VALUES ?txid { 2131053 2204314 2282865 }
  ?tx a amd:FundsTransfer ; amd:transferId ?txid ; ?term ?v .
}
The evidence query. ?term is a variable that layer 3 fills in. No table or column is named anywhere in it.

Read it as: for every indicator of T0039, take the AML terms it needs (layer 3), and for each of the three transfers give me the value of each of those terms.

Step 3 — OBDA query rewriting

The application issues the semantic query. Ontop uses the ontology and mappings to rewrite it into SQL, which is executed against DuckDB in read-only mode. The investigator does not need to know the physical table and column names. A smaller query shows the three hops of the rewriting (layer 2 term → layer 4 bridge → layer 5 column):

PREFIX amd: <https://example.org/aml-data#>
SELECT ?from WHERE { ?tx a amd:FundsTransfer ; amd:transferId 2131053 ; amd:originator ?from }
Asks for amd:originator, an AML term of layer 2.
SELECT "v1"."from_account" AS "from_account1m15"
FROM "tx_features" "v1"
WHERE ("v1"."from_account" IS NOT NULL AND 2131053 = "v1"."tx_id")
The SQL Ontop wrote and ran: amd:originator → fcd:fromAccount (bridge) → column from_account (R2RML). Result: account 812D22980.

Two things I observed when running it:

  • Without the ontology the same query silently returns zero rows. I ran it with the bridge left out: no error, no rows. Ontop treats an unknown term as “no data”, not as a mistake. This is why every term in a model-written query is checked against the known vocabulary before it reaches Ontop.
  • amd:Jurisdiction returns zero rows too, with the bridge present. The system does not read “no country” from the empty result; it reads it from the missing bridge line (step 5).

The full evidence query is larger. Because the predicate is a variable, Ontop expands ?term to every term of every T0039 indicator that has a bridge, joins in the typology facts, and writes one statement of about 15,000 characters with 39 UNION branches. It ran in 2.4 seconds on the 5-million-row table and returned 12 rows, one for each transfer and term. I have not tried it on many transfers, so I do not know how it scales.

Step 4 — Evidence evaluation

The returned transactions are ordered and assembled into a candidate flow. Plain code does this: one row for each transfer, sorted by time, the first payer taken as the origin. No interpretation happens here.

@prefix ex: <https://example.org/rules#> .
@prefix xsd: <http://www.w3.org/2001/XMLSchema#> .

<https://example.org/candidate> a ex:LoopCandidate ;
    ex:leg <https://example.org/transaction/2131053>,
        <https://example.org/transaction/2204314>,
        <https://example.org/transaction/2282865> ;
    ex:origin <https://example.org/account/812D22980> .

<https://example.org/transaction/2131053> a ex:Leg ;
    ex:at "2022-09-04T04:53:00"^^xsd:dateTime ;
    ex:from <https://example.org/account/812D22980> ;
    ex:sameBank false ;
    ex:seq 1 ;
    ex:to <https://example.org/account/811C597B0> .

<https://example.org/transaction/2204314> a ex:Leg ;
    ex:at "2022-09-04T14:05:00"^^xsd:dateTime ;
    ex:from <https://example.org/account/811C597B0> ;
    ex:sameBank false ;
    ex:seq 2 ;
    ex:to <https://example.org/account/811B83280> .

<https://example.org/transaction/2282865> a ex:Leg ;
    ex:at "2022-09-04T23:49:00"^^xsd:dateTime ;
    ex:from <https://example.org/account/811B83280> ;
    ex:sameBank false ;
    ex:seq 3 ;
    ex:to <https://example.org/account/812D22980> .
The candidate loop that SHACL receives.

The rule implementation checks the defined circular structure, including connectivity, multiple accounts and time order. For the three transfers all seven rules of IND02191 pass. To see that the shape is more than a rubber stamp, I removed the middle transfer (2204314) and ran it again: consecutive_legs_connect fails, because the second leg no longer starts where the first one ended, and the indicator becomes not held. The rule definition is a project interpretation and should be reviewed by AML practitioners.

Known loose end: the SHACL leg shape still asks for a logarithmic amount that this mode no longer fetches. The indicator's rule list leaves that rule out, so the verdict is not affected, but the raw SHACL result contains it.

Step 5 — Indicator outcomes

The demo reports IND02191 as a structure match, IND02195 as facts only, and the other seven indicators as cannot check here because required information is not available in the connected dataset. The last part is decided by comparing what each indicator needs (layer 3) with the AML terms that have a bridge (layer 4). No empty query result is involved.

IndicatorDemo outcomeInterpretation
IND02191Structure matchesThe transfers form a circular structure and return to the original account. A legitimate business rationale is not established by this structural match.
IND02195Facts onlyThree hops were observed, all between banks, but the typology does not define a numerical threshold for multiple hops, and the data has no country.
Other sevenCannot check hereRequired invoice, shipment, jurisdiction, company or other information is unavailable in the connected dataset: those amd: terms have no bridge.

Step 6 — Explanation

The LLM generates a natural-language summary from the structured findings. The application constrains the summary to the returned facts and verdicts, with a deterministic fallback if unsupported claims are detected.

If the bank's table changes, only layer 5 and the right-hand side of layer 4 change. If a new indicator needs another AML term, one line in layer 3 changes. If the typology is updated, layer 1 changes. A reviewer can read the file for their layer; nobody has to read a SQL query to find out what a question meant.

6. Results: what has actually been verified?

The current implementation includes 26 gold test cases covering transaction loops and account background. Ontop query results match hand-written SQL across the 26 cases. SHACL results agree with the gold verdicts across the 19 loop cases. The project reports 144 automated tests, including checks on semantic-layer organisation. In the worked example, retrieval takes approximately two seconds.

These are engineering checks of the implemented pipeline and its test fixtures. They do not establish that every AML interpretation or indicator rule is correct. The current model has not yet undergone formal AML domain-expert review and the demonstration covers one technique. It is not yet evidence of production readiness or of improved investigator outcomes.

One more limit, specific to this mode: the model's SPARQL is close to a template (only the technique and the ids change). So the setup does not test whether a small model can write hard SPARQL; it tests whether the model can pick the technique and extract the ids, and whether the rest of the chain holds.

7. Challenges and lessons learned

Semantic modelling is harder than query generation.

The difficult work is defining what concepts mean, which relationships are valid and how requirements should be reused. For example, an originator party and a source account are distinct concepts.

Data availability is not evidence sufficiency.

A field can exist without being semantically equivalent to the required concept, sufficiently complete or fit for the intended conclusion. Coverage should distinguish semantic coverage, mapping coverage, physical availability and data quality.

Not everything belongs in an ontology.

An ontology, an R2RML mapping, a SHACL shape, a business rule and runtime configuration have different responsibilities. Turtle is a serialization format, not a guarantee that all files represent the same kind of model.

Deterministic does not automatically mean correct.

A rule can execute consistently while encoding an incorrect assumption. Explicit rules are reviewable, but still require domain validation.

Governance becomes important as the model grows.

Extending the approach to more techniques and data sources will require ownership, versioning, provenance, mapping governance and impact analysis.

8. Final thoughts: semantic layers as a foundation for Agentic AI

Much of the discussion about Agentic AI focuses on agents, orchestration, tools and increasingly capable models. An equally important question is what organisational knowledge those agents can access, and how we ensure that they use it correctly.

In this experiment, semantic knowledge defines the AML vocabulary and typology requirements; semantic data access connects those concepts to physical data; and deterministic validation evaluates evidence against explicit rules. The LLM sits above these capabilities, interpreting intent and communicating findings.

The future of trustworthy enterprise AI is not just about building smarter agents. It is also about giving those agents a well-defined understanding of the business they operate in.
